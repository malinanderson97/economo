0How to read this document

Economo is mapped closely to Olivier Blanchard's Macroeconomics (9th ed., 2024). Where the tool departs from or extends the textbook, the departure is identified in the section where it occurs and the reasoning given.

How to read this document

Every section has the same three layers:

  1. The main text gives the equation the tool uses and its derivation from the textbook, shown line by line.
  2. Values the model uses opens to show each parameter's default and why that value was chosen.
  3. How this appears in the code opens to show the equation beside the lines of the engine that compute it, with the names the code uses for each symbol.

Each equation carries one of four labels:

  • Blanchard — taken directly from the textbook.
  • Blanchard, rearranged — the textbook's relation in a different algebraic form (for example, converted from unemployment to output, or solved for Y).
  • Blanchard, linearised — the relation is Blanchard's, written in a specific linear form: his own where he gives one, the tool's derivation where he does not.
  • Tool addition — something the textbook does not contain, which the tool needs in order to run.

Where a label says Tool departure — calibration, the form is Blanchard's but the tool assigns a value the textbook's structure would not, and says why.

A note on unlocking

The tool is taught in stages, and some mechanisms are inactive until their block is reached: expected inflation before the Phillips-curve block, imports and exports before the open-economy block, and the Taylor rule until switched on. Where this changes what an equation does, the section says so.

1Approach

1.1Linearisations

Many of the equations Blanchard uses are not given exact forms; they are shown as functions, with directions of movement. To run, the tool must assign a value to every parameter, so Economo's engine is built from linearisations of all the equations and parameters. Where Blanchard offers an example linear equation it has been used; elsewhere the linear form has had to be developed.

Blanchard treats linearising a behavioural relation as a reasonable simplification and says so for consumption: taking consumption and disposable income to be related by C = c₀ + c₁Y_D (eq. 3.2) leaves the relation characterised by just two parameters, and he adds that a model nearly always begins with "Assume", signalling that reality is being simplified to focus on the issue at hand (§3-2, pp. 46–47). He does it himself for wage setting (§8-1, p. 158) and for investment (Ch. 5, Problem 2). The one relation the tool linearises that he leaves in general form is net exports; that step is derived and badged in §7.

Each linearisation is set out in the section where it is used.

1.2Value calibration

The model has no growth. The medium run in Blanchard is not about the level of output rising over time but about how the economy returns to potential after a shock, so the questions the tool can answer are about stability: how far a shock moves the economy, and for how long. This is not a small subject. A central bank's ability to hold inflation at target, and to keep expectations anchored to it, is the difference between a shock that passes in two periods and one that persists. The Phillips-curve block, with its dynamically changing expectations, is built to show this.

Because the tool shows movements around a central point, every value is chosen so that potential output is

Yₙ = 100

and so that the economy sits there when the real interest rate equals the natural rate of 1% — the full model's opening state. In the earlier stages, where the policy rate is nominal, set at 3%, and expected inflation is not yet active, the real rate is 3% and output opens below potential (§2, Values). Where Blanchard supplies a number it is used; where he does not, the value is chosen to produce a clean composition of output at this calibration point. Each choice is explained after the derivation it belongs to.

2.2Investment

I = b₀ + b₁Y − b₂r (Ch. 5, Problem 2; eq. 9.1) Blanchard, linearised

Blanchard writes investment as I(Y, i) in eq. 5.1 and, from Chapter 6 onward, in terms of the real rate; in eq. 9.1 the argument is I(Y, r + x). The risk premium x is introduced in §6-2 (pp. 114–115) as the premium a borrower pays over the policy rate, and first enters the IS relation in eq. 6.5 (p. 121), which eq. 9.1 repeats. The linear form is the one Blanchard himself uses in Ch. 5, Problem 2, with r in place of i as in eq. 9.1.

The tool sets x = 0. Nothing in the model determines a risk premium: that would require debt dynamics and default risk, for which there is no state variable here. In the closed stages a student can see the demand-side effect of a higher borrowing cost by raising the policy rate, since only r + x enters the IS; that substitution does not carry over to the open stages, where a higher policy rate appreciates the currency through UIP whereas higher country risk would depreciate it.

2.3The real interest rate

Before the Phillips-curve block unlocks, expectations play no role, so πᵉ = 0 and the tool shows r = i. Blanchard states this himself as the first implication of eq. 6.4: when expected inflation is zero, the nominal and real rates are equal (p. 112). It is not a separate relation and carries no equation number of its own.

Once the block is active the tool uses the Fisher relation:

(2.5)

r ≈ i − πᵉ (eq. 6.4) Blanchard

Blanchard gives the exact form in eq. 6.3 and notes that eq. 6.4 is only an approximation, valid while both rates are small, though he treats it as an equality for the rest of the book. The tool does the same: it uses the linear form throughout. At the rates the tool operates in, the difference between eq. 6.3 and eq. 6.4 is invisible on the chart.

This is the closed-economy multiplier, k = 1/(1 − c₁ − b₁). It is Blanchard's Ch. 3 multiplier 1/(1 − c₁) with the investment accelerator b₁ added to the induced-spending term, because from Chapter 5 onward investment also responds to output.

2.6Net exports and the open multiplier

In the closed stages of the tool there are no imports or exports: m₁ = 0 and the trade term is absent, so the closed form above is the whole equation. In the open stages the trade term x₁Y* − n₁(ε − 1) is added to the bracket and the denominator gains + m₁, giving k_o = 1/(1 − c₁ − b₁ + m₁).

Two forms of the same relation. The trade term in the bracket carries no −m₁Y, while §7 ends with NX = x₁Y* − m₁Y − n₁(ε − 1). Both are correct; they are the same relation before and after Y has been solved out.

The behavioural form is what the equation box in the tool displays: imports rise with domestic output, so the −m₁Y term is present.

NX = x₁Y* − m₁Y − n₁(ε − 1)

The solved form is what the engine computes. Because m₁Y depends on Y, it is collected with the other Y terms:

(2.8)

Y(1 − c₁ − b₁ + m₁) = c₀ − c₁T + b₀ − b₂r + G + x₁Y* − n₁(ε − 1)

so the term the engine calls trade holds only the two exogenous pieces, x₁Y* − n₁(ε − 1), and the import leakage has become the + m₁ in the denominator of k_o.

This is why m₁ carries a minus sign in the NX equation but a plus sign in the multiplier: it is the same leakage seen from either side of the rearrangement. A higher marginal propensity to import lowers every multiplier in the open economy, since part of any rise in demand leaks abroad (Blanchard Ch. 18). The NX equation itself, and the derivation of n₁, are given in §7.

How the tool's multipliers compare with Blanchard's. Blanchard's Ch. 3 multiplier is 2.5 at c₁ = 0.6 (p. 53). The tool's is larger in the closed economy — k = 1/(1 − 0.6 − 0.1) = 3.33, because investment also responds to output — and smaller in the open economy — k_o = 1/(1 − 0.6 − 0.1 + 0.3) = 1.67, because imports leak. His own note on that page says empirical multipliers are typically smaller than 2.5, because the Ch. 3 model leaves out the monetary-policy reaction and the fact that some demand falls on foreign goods — both of which this model has.

Values the model usesdefaults and why they were chosen

The calibration point. Every IS value is set so that output equals potential, Y = Yₙ = 100, when the real rate equals the natural rate of 1%. Blanchard's Table 3-1 (p. 44) gives the composition of US GDP in 2022 as C 68.1%, I 17.5%, G 17.4%, NX −3.8%. The tool rounds this to C 60, I 20, G 20, NX 0. Government spending is rounded up to 20 and taxes set equal to it, so the budget is balanced at baseline; net exports are set to zero so that trade is balanced at the calibration point; investment is rounded up to 20; consumption is the residual. Each intercept — c₀, b₀ — is then solved so that its component hits its share at the calibration point.

Opening output. In the first two stages of the tool the policy rate is nominal and set at 3%, and with no expected inflation the real rate is also 3% — above the natural rate. Output therefore opens below potential: Y = 86.7 in the closed IS-LM stage and 93.3 in the open IS-LM-UIP stage, where the shortfall in output holds imports down and net exports open at +2.00. This is Blanchard's short run: output below potential because the policy rate is above the natural rate. Once the Phillips-curve block is active, expected inflation of 2% brings the real rate to 1% and output to 100.

c₁ = 0.6 (slider, 0.30–0.80). Blanchard puts the propensity to consume at around 0.6, noting that the Appendix 3 regressions yield two estimates, 0.5 and 0.8 (§3-3, p. 53). The tool uses his 0.6. The slider spans 0.30–0.80.

b₁ = 0.1. Chapter 5 makes investment respond to output but assigns no coefficient in the text: eq. 5.1 gives only the sign, and Ch. 5 Problem 2 writes I = b₀ + b₁Y − b₂i with b₁ unspecified. Problem 5 (pp. 105–106) does set I = 150 + 0.25Y − 1000i, but its b₁ = 0.25 is paired with c₁ = 0.25, giving k = 2; combined with the text's c₁ = 0.6 it would give a closed-economy multiplier of 4. b₁ = 0.1 is a tool calibration, small enough that c₁ + b₁ = 0.7 stays well inside the stability bound.

The stability bound and the slider cap. Blanchard restricts 0 < c₁ < 1 (§3-2, p. 47); Ch. 5 Problem 2 generalises this to c₁ + b₁ < 1. The multiplier 1/(1 − c₁ − b₁) diverges as c₁ + b₁ → 1. The slider is capped at c₁ = 0.8, where c₁ + b₁ = 0.9 and k = 10, so the divergent region is unreachable. The cap coincides with the higher of his two reported estimates and keeps the multiplier at a teachable size; stability alone would allow c₁ up to about 0.89.

c₀ = 12. Blanchard defines c₀ as consumption when disposable income is zero (§3-2, p. 47) but assigns it no value; his consumption regression is in first differences and carries no intercept. The tool solves eq. 3.3 for c₀ at the calibration point, with C = 60:

60 = c₀ + 0.6 × (100 − 20)

60 = c₀ + 48

c₀ = 12

b₂ = 200. Blanchard gives the rate channel its sign (eq. 5.1) but no coefficient in the text; Problem 5's b₂ = 1000, in an economy of Y = 1000, is a 1%-of-output investment response per point, half the tool's 2%. He does say (Ch. 16, pp. 336–338) that the output effect of a rate change is small if only the current rate moves and expectations of future rates do not, and much larger if the change is expected to persist. A policy-rate change in the tool is persistent — the slider is held at its new level — so b₂ is calibrated to the larger case: a one-point change in r moves investment by 2, a tenth of baseline investment. In the full model it moves output by about 3.7: about 3.3 through the multiplier on investment, and about 0.34 through the exchange rate. The exchange-rate figure takes two steps, and the numbers in them are different objects. A one-point rate differential appreciates the currency by about 1% (§8); that cuts net exports by n₁ × 0.01 ≈ 0.21 (§7); and the multiplier then carries that fall into output, 0.21 × k_o ≈ 0.35. The engine measures 0.34, because the appreciation at a 1pp differential is 0.97% rather than a full point. In the closed economy the response is 6.7, all through the multiplier. Across the policy-rate range of 0–15% output runs from about 111 to about 56. This sensitivity is carried into §5, where it is the reason ψ is set at half Taylor's value.

b₀ = 12. With b₁ and b₂ fixed, b₀ follows from the investment anchor I = 20 at Y = 100, r = 1%:

20 = b₀ + 0.1 × 100 − 200 × 0.01

20 = b₀ + 10 − 2

b₀ = 12

G = 20, T = 20. Government spending is rounded from Blanchard's 17.4% share to 20% of potential output. Taxes are set equal to it so that the budget is balanced at baseline (T − G = 0; §3-3, p. 50), and both are sliders so the user can move either side of the budget independently.

function openOn() {
  return currentStage().economy === 'open';
}

function isOutput(G, T, r, eps, c1, m1, Ystar) {
  const c  = (c1    !== undefined) ? c1    : 0.6;
  const m  = openOn() ? ((m1 !== undefined) ? m1 : 0.30) : 0;
  const Ys = (Ystar !== undefined) ? Ystar : YSTAR_BASE;

3The LM curve: the policy rate

Textbook anchor: Ch. 5 (§5-3, p. 94), Ch. 6 (eq. 6.4, p. 112), Ch. 9 (rₙ), Ch. 23 (§23-2, p. 494), Ch. 4 (§4-4, p. 79)

A note on notation. Blanchard uses ī for two different things. In §5-3 it is the interest rate the central bank chooses, so the LM curve is the horizontal line i = ī. In §23-2 the same symbol is the target nominal rate in the Taylor rule — the rate that goes with the neutral real rate rₙ and the inflation target. The tool follows the Ch. 23 usage: i is the policy rate the central bank sets (the slider), and ī is the rate this document calls the neutral nominal rate, which is Blanchard's target nominal rate. This section is where the two meet.

3.1The policy rate

Across Chapters 5 and 9, Blanchard's LM curve is flat: the central bank chooses the nominal interest rate and supplies whatever money is needed to hold it there. This is reflected in the tool:

(3.1)

i = the policy rate set by the central bank (§23-2, p. 494) Blanchard

Blanchard calls i "the policy rate, that is, the nominal interest rate controlled by the central bank" (p. 494). It is written here without a bar: §5-3 states the same relation as i = ī, but that bar means the rate the central bank has chosen, which is the opposite of the way the bar is used in the rest of this section. In the tool that rate is the policy-rate slider, and when the Taylor rule is off the slider is the policy rate: no smoothing, no lag. When the Taylor rule is on, the slider is inactive and the rule of §5 sets the rate each period.

The default policy rate is 3%. This is not arbitrary: it is the neutral nominal rate at baseline, rₙ + π̄ = 1% + 2%, derived in 3.2. It is why the full model opens exactly at potential output, and why the earlier stages, where expected inflation is not yet active and the real rate is therefore 3%, open below it (§2, Values).

3.2The neutral nominal rate

(3.2)

ī = rₙ + π̄ (§23-2, p. 494) Blanchard

The tool calls ī the neutral nominal rate: the rate the central bank should set when the economy is balanced, output at potential and inflation at target. Blanchard calls the same object the target nominal interest rate — "the nominal interest rate associated with the neutral rate of interest, rₙ, and the target rate of inflation" (§23-2, p. 494); for him "neutral" attaches to the real rate rₙ. It is rₙ plus target inflation, by the Fisher relation of eq. 6.4. When the Taylor rule is active, the central bank adjusts up or down from this rate according to the inflation and output gaps (§5); when the rule is off, the neutral rate is still computed each period and, once the Phillips-curve block is unlocked, displayed in the equation box's Taylor line, greyed out, so the user can see how far the chosen rate is from it.

How the engine finds rₙ. rₙ is not stored as a constant. The tool computes it by running the IS relation backwards. Forwards, the IS gives output for a given rate:

Y = k_o · [ c₀ − c₁T + b₀ − b₂r + G + trade ]

Backwards, output is fixed at Yₙ and the equation is solved for the rate that produces it. Collect every term except the interest-rate channel into A:

(3.3)

A = c₀ − c₁T + b₀ + G + trade

Yₙ = k_o · [ A − b₂rₙ ]

Yₙ / k_o = A − b₂rₙ

b₂rₙ = A − Yₙ / k_o

rₙ = ( A − Yₙ / k_o ) / b₂ Tool addition — mechanism

So rₙ is the real rate at which the IS curve passes through Y = Yₙ: the rate that makes demand equal potential output. This is the rate Blanchard describes in §9-2 (p. 183): the real rate at which the demand for goods equals potential output, called the natural, neutral or Wicksellian rate of interest. The tool's rₙ is that object. The neutral nominal rate then follows from the Fisher relation: ī = rₙ + π̄.

At baseline (full model): A = 12 − 12 + 12 + 20 + 30 = 62, k_o = 1/0.6, Yₙ = 100 so Yₙ/k_o = 60, and rₙ = (62 − 60) / 200 = 0.01, giving ī = 1% + 2% = 3%.

What follows from this. A contains G, T, ε and Y*, so rₙ moves whenever any of them move. Raise G and demand at any given rate is higher, so the rate needed to hold output at Yₙ must be higher too, and rₙ rises. A real depreciation does the same through the trade term. This is why the neutral rate shown in the equation box shifts when a fiscal or exchange-rate slider is dragged, even though nothing labelled "interest rate" has been touched.

Blanchard defines the natural rate as a concept but does not give this construction. Solving the IS relation at Y = Yₙ is therefore a tool addition in mechanism, though the object it produces is the textbook's.

3.3The zero lower bound

(3.4)

i = max(0, i) (§4-4, p. 79) Blanchard

The nominal interest rate cannot go below zero, a constraint Blanchard names the zero lower bound in the section on the liquidity trap and returns to in Ch. 23. The tool applies it to the policy rate however it is set: the slider cannot be moved below 0%, and when the Taylor rule calls for a negative rate the rate is held at zero instead. The tool uses a strict zero bound, as Blanchard does. He notes on the same page that people and firms will hold some bonds at slightly negative rates, citing Germany, and calls it another complication he ignores (§4-4, p. 79).

4The Phillips curve

Textbook anchor: Ch. 7 (eqs. 7.1, 7.3), Ch. 8 (eqs. 8.1–8.5; §8-3), Ch. 9 (p. 179, eq. 9.3)

The equation the tool uses:

π = πᵉ + α(1 − uₙ)(Y − Yₙ)/Yₙ + shock [Blanchard, rearranged; shock is a tool addition]

4.0Where the Phillips curve comes from — Chapter 8

Blanchard derives the Phillips curve from wage and price setting. Wage setting is W = Pᵉ F(u, z) (eq. 7.1, §7-3, p. 143). The specific functional form F(u, z) = 1 − αu + z is the one he adopts in §8-1 (p. 158), where it is written out but carries no equation number:

(4.1)

W = Pᵉ(1 − αu + z) (eq. 7.1 with F(u, z) = 1 − αu + z; §8-1, p. 158) Blanchard

Price setting (eq. 7.3): firms set price as a markup over the wage:

(4.2)

P = (1 + m)W (eq. 7.3) Blanchard

Substituting the wage into the price equation gives the price level in terms of the expected price level, the markup, the wage-push factor, and unemployment:

P = Pᵉ(1 + m)(1 − αu + z) (eq. 8.1) Blanchard

Rewritten in inflation terms (derivation in Blanchard's chapter appendix), with time indexes:

(4.3)

πₜ = πᵉₜ + (m + z) − αuₜ (eq. 8.3) Blanchard

The two supply-side terms are distinct objects: m is the markup of prices over wages (market power), and z is everything in wage setting other than unemployment (bargaining power, unemployment benefits, employment protection). The tool exposes them as two separate sliders.

The natural rate of unemployment is the rate at which actual inflation equals expected inflation. Setting πₜ = πᵉₜ in eq. 8.3 and solving:

(4.4)

uₙ = (m + z)/α (eq. 8.4) Blanchard

A rise in either m or z raises uₙ, and so lowers potential output Yₙ = L(1 − uₙ). Substituting eq. 8.4 back into eq. 8.3 gives the Phillips curve in unemployment space:

(4.5)

πₜ − πᵉₜ = −α(uₜ − uₙ) (eq. 8.5) Blanchard

4.1The unemployment–output relation

Blanchard converts eq. 8.5 to output space in Chapter 9. Two equations from p. 179:

(4.6)

(A) Yₙ = L(1 − uₙ) Blanchard

and

(4.7)

(B) π − πᵉ = (α/L)(Y − Yₙ) (eq. 9.3) Blanchard

Rearrange (A) for L:

L = Yₙ/(1 − uₙ)

Substitute for L in (B):

π − πᵉ = α(1 − uₙ)(Y − Yₙ)/Yₙ Blanchard, rearranged

Blanchard's eq. 9.3 normalises the output gap by the labour force L; the tool normalises by potential output Yₙ, so that every block shares the same denominator (Yₙ = 100). The (1 − uₙ) factor is the exact cost of that swap. It is kept in full and computed from the current uₙ, not fixed, so α in the tool's equation is exactly Blanchard's α — nothing is approximated. At baseline uₙ = 0.05 the factor is 0.95.

4.2Supply-side additions

Blanchard's eq. 9.3 has no shock term. The tool's Phillips curve is

(4.8)

π = πᵉ + α(1 − uₙ)(Y − Yₙ)/Yₙ + shock Tool addition — the shock term

where shock is a tool addition (cf. Blanchard §8-3 on the oil-price and Covid episodes). It is distinct from the structural supply side, and the two must not be confused:

  • The structural terms are Blanchard's own: the markup m and wage-push z of eq. 8.4, uₙ = (m + z)/α. Raising either raises uₙ and so lowers Yₙ = L(1 − uₙ). These are sliders in the tool ("Markup, m" and "Wage push, z") and change where the economy settles.

  • The shock term shifts the Phillips curve up without touching uₙ or Yₙ. It comes in two forms: a persistent shift set by the "Cost-push shock (PC shift)" slider, and a transitory pulse fired by the oil-shock button (an initial +5pp that decays by a factor of 0.7 each period). Both are exogenous and both leave the medium-run equilibrium unchanged; they change the path back to it, not the destination.

The distinction matters for reading the tool: a markup or wage-push change is a shift in natural output, so π ≠ πᵉ persists until the economy has moved to the new Yₙ; a cost-push shock is a disturbance around an unchanged Yₙ, so inflation returns to target once the shock has passed and expectations have re-anchored.

The episodes are Blanchard's (§8-3): the 2020–22 inflation came largely through a temporary rise in the markup (energy prices and supply-chain shortages raised costs given wages) and faded when commodity prices turned; the 1970s oil shocks, by contrast, de-anchored expectations and turned a transitory shock into sustained inflation. The tool can run the same pulse both ways, depending on the credibility settings of §6.

The form is the tool's. In Blanchard a temporary markup rise enters through eq. 8.4 and so temporarily raises uₙ and lowers Yₙ (Ch. 9, p. 185); the tool instead adds the shock to inflation directly and holds Yₙ fixed, so that a transitory disturbance and a permanent change in m remain visibly distinct on the chart.

4.3One symbol, two values: α

In Blanchard there is a single α. It is the slope of the wage-setting function on p. 158, and the same symbol appears in both eq. 8.4, uₙ = (m + z)/α, and eq. 8.5, the Phillips-curve slope. The tool uses two numbers for it: a fixed α_ws = 3.0 inside uₙ = (m + z)/α_ws, and the PC-slope slider α = 0.3 in the Phillips curve. Moving the slider never changes uₙ or Yₙ. Tool departure — calibration

Blanchard is not wrong to use one α: in the textbook it is never assigned a number, so one parameter governing both the natural rate and the slope is exactly the right theoretical structure. The conflict appears only when the tool, being quantitative, must make that one α hit two empirical targets at once. With the tool's baseline m + z = 0.15, the natural-rate formula needs α ≈ 3 to give a plausible uₙ of 5%; an α of 0.3 there would imply 50% unemployment. But the estimated Phillips-curve slope is about 0.3, ten times smaller; an α of 3 there would move inflation by nearly three points for a 1% output gap. The standard resolution, and the tool's, is that price stickiness and real rigidities flatten the observed inflation–output relation far below the underlying wage-setting sensitivity, so once calibrated the two coefficients are different objects. This is a deliberate calibration split, not a correction of a textbook error.

Values the model usesdefaults and why they were chosen

Markup m = 0.05 and wage push z = 0.10 (sliders, each 0.01–0.30); α_ws = 3.0 (fixed). Blanchard gives none of these a number. They are set jointly so that uₙ = (0.05 + 0.10)/3 = 0.05, a natural rate of 5%. Blanchard gives no figure for the natural rate but says that over a long enough period the average unemployment rate is a good proxy for it (§7-5, p. 150), and reports the US average over 2000–2020 as 6.0% (Focus box, p. 149); the tool's 5% sits just below that. m and z are exposed as separate sliders because they are distinct objects in Blanchard (market power versus wage-setting institutions) and because they are substitutes in eq. 8.4: a markup rise can be offset by a fall in z, restoring Yₙ — a genuine medium-run rebalancing.

Labour force L = 100/(1 − 0.05) ≈ 105.26 (fixed). Set so that Yₙ = L(1 − uₙ) = 100 at the baseline natural rate. L does not move; when m or z change uₙ, it is Yₙ that moves.

PC slope α = 0.3 (slider, 0.05–0.50). The tool's α multiplies an unemployment gap in a relation anchored on expected inflation, so the comparable empirical object is the coefficient on unemployment in a Phillips curve written with expectations as the anchor. Hazell, Herreño, Nakamura and Steinsson (QJE 2022) estimate exactly that: a one-point rise in unemployment lowers inflation by about 0.34 points. That figure is their persistence-scaled slope coefficient, not the structural slope — it is built from a structural slope of 0.0062 (quarterly, non-tradeable goods), weighted against their separate estimate for rents and scaled by the persistence of unemployment fluctuations, then annualised. Across their specifications it runs from about 0.09 to 0.42, so 0.3 sits inside the estimated range rather than at a point estimate, and the slider's 0.05–0.50 spans it with room either side. A slope near 1 is not supported by any of these estimates, which is why the slider is capped at 0.5.

The same paper supports the tool's treatment of the 1970s: its central finding is that the sharp fall in core inflation in the early 1980s came mostly from shifting expectations about long-run monetary policy rather than from a steep Phillips curve, and that the stability of inflation after 1990 reflects long-run expectations becoming more firmly anchored. This is Blanchard's account in different language — he attributes the apparently steep 1970s curve to the expectations parameter θ rising toward 1 (eqs. 8.5 → 8.8 → 8.9; Figure 8-4), not to a larger α. The tool follows both, so steepness over time is produced by de-anchoring (§6), not by the α slider.

No textbook source for the sizes; they are chosen to be clearly visible on the chart and, for the pulse, to fade within a few periods as Blanchard describes the Covid episode fading. The form — a pulse added to inflation that decays while Yₙ stays fixed — is the tool's; Blanchard's temporary markup rise moves uₙ and Yₙ for as long as it lasts (Ch. 9, p. 185).

5The Taylor rule

Textbook anchor: Ch. 23 (§23-2, p. 494), Ch. 9 (p. 179)

The rule the tool applies each period, when the Taylor rule is switched on — solved in the same period as output and inflation, as on p. 494:

iₜ = ρ·iₜ₋₁ + (1 − ρ)·[ ī + φ(πₜ − π̄) + ψ(Yₜ − Yₙ)/Yₙ ] [Blanchard, rearranged; smoothing ρ is a tool addition]

with ī the neutral nominal rate of §3.2 and, as always, iₜ ≥ 0 (§3.3).

5.1From Blanchard's rule to the tool's

Blanchard sets out the rule Taylor proposed for the central bank (p. 494):

(5.1)

(A) iₜ = ī + a(πₜ − π̄) − b(uₜ − uₙ) Blanchard

with a, b > 0 and, "as Taylor pointed out, a should be larger than one." This is the Taylor principle: spending depends on the real rate, so the nominal rate must rise more than one-for-one with inflation for the real rate to rise at all.

The rule is written in the unemployment gap; the tool works in the output gap. From p. 179:

(B) Yₙ = L(1 − uₙ)

(C) Y = L(1 − u)

Subtract (B) from (C):

Y − Yₙ = L(1 − u) − L(1 − uₙ)

Y − Yₙ = L(uₙ − u)

Multiply both sides by −1 and divide by L:

(5.2)

(D) (Yₙ − Y)/L = (u − uₙ)

Substitute (D) into (A):

(E) iₜ = ī + a(πₜ − π̄) − (b/L)(Yₙ − Y)

Rearrange (B) for L, L = Yₙ/(1 − uₙ), and substitute into (E):

iₜ = ī + a(πₜ − π̄) − b(1 − uₙ)(Yₙ − Y)/Yₙ

Pull the minus inside the bracket:

iₜ = ī + a(πₜ − π̄) + b(1 − uₙ)(Y − Yₙ)/Yₙ Blanchard, rearranged

The sign of the gap term has flipped because u and Y move oppositely; the (1 − uₙ) factor is the same one as in the Phillips curve (§4.1), for the same reason. Neither is a disagreement with Blanchard — both are the conversion.

5.2Interest-rate smoothing

The rule the engine applies each period is

(5.4)

iₜ = ρ·iₜ₋₁ + (1 − ρ)·[ ī + φ(π − π̄) + ψ(Y − Yₙ)/Yₙ ] Tool addition

The bracket is the Taylor rule from 5.1. The new rate is a weighted average of last period's rate (weight ρ) and the rate the rule would set (weight 1 − ρ): the central bank closes a fraction (1 − ρ) of the gap to its target rate each period. Blanchard's rule has no lagged-rate term. Two things this does and one it does not:

  • It reflects reality. Estimated Taylor rules on central-bank data fit poorly without a lagged rate term and find ρ of roughly 0.7–0.9 quarterly (Clarida, Galí and Gertler, 1998, 2000). Central banks move in steps over several meetings; that is partial adjustment.

  • It is a gradualism dial, not a stability device. Because ī is recomputed from the current IS position (§3.2), the bracket of 5.4 already contains the rate that would put output back at potential. With ρ = 0 the central bank jumps to it at once: after a fiscal shock of G + 5 in the closed IS-LM-PC stage the rate goes straight to 5.50% and output stays at Yₙ in every period — the demand shock is fully offset on impact, with no output or inflation response at all. That is the divine-coincidence result, and it is correct, but it shows the student nothing. With ρ = 0.75 the bank closes a quarter of the distance each period — 4.62%, 5.19%, 5.39%, 5.46%, 5.49%, 5.50% — and the adjustment is visible: output 105.9 → 102.1 → 100.7 → 100.3 → 100.1 → 100.0, within half a point of potential from the third period. This is the gradualism of flexible inflation targeting (§23-2): the bank trades a slower return for a smoother rate path. Nor is the slower return paid for when inflation is the problem: after the oil shock at θ = 0.75 and φ = 1.5, inflation is back within 0.1pp of target at period 12 with ρ = 0.75 but only at period 22 with ρ = 0, because the inertia keeps the rate up while expected inflation unwinds.

  • It does not weaken the rule. Smoothing changes the path of the response, not its size. A persistent inflation overshoot still receives the full φ(π − π̄) response once the adjustment has worked through, so the Taylor principle is preserved. This is why smoothing is a separate parameter rather than a smaller φ or ψ: a smaller φ would permanently reduce the response and, below 1, break the principle the slider exists to demonstrate.

  const gap = (eq.Y - eq.Y_n) / eq.Y_n;

    if (excess(mid) > 0) lo = mid; else hi = mid;
  }
  return solveAtRate(s, 0.5 * (lo + hi));
}

6Inflation expectations and anchoring

Textbook anchor: Ch. 8 (eq. 8.7, p. 161; §8-4, p. 169; §8-3, p. 165), Ch. 9 (§9-2, pp. 184–185)

The rule the tool applies each period:

(6.2)

λₜ = (1 − θ)·credₜ

(6.3)

πᵉₜ₊₁ = πᵉₜ + s·[ λₜ(π̄ − πᵉₜ) + (1 − λₜ)(πₜ − πᵉₜ) ] [Blanchard eq. 8.7, with two tool additions: cred and s]

with cred updated each period by the law of motion in 6.4.

6.1Blanchard's expectations rule

In eq. 8.7 Blanchard splits inflation expectations between the target rate stated by the central bank and the previous period's inflation:

(6.1)

πᵉ = (1 − θ)π̄ + θπₜ₋₁ (eq. 8.7) Blanchard

θ = 0 is full anchoring (πᵉ = π̄); θ = 1 is fully adaptive (πᵉ = πₜ₋₁). Blanchard describes how expectations can de-anchor over time when inflation repeatedly fails to match the target: "Suppose that inflation lasts for a while and leads wage setters to … start adjusting their inflation expectations to reflect actual inflation" (p. 165). De-anchoring is a rise in θ from 0 toward 1, driven by inflation remaining above target for some time; the risk grows with the length of the overshoot, and a more credible starting point makes it less likely (pp. 184–185). His own worked case has θ jump straight from 0 to 1 to keep the algebra simple.

6.2The two layers

To let expectations de-anchor gradually while leaving the user in control, the tool splits anchoring into two factors:

  • θ is Blanchard's adaptive weight from eq. 8.7, set by the user. The tool treats (1 − θ) as the ceiling on anchoring: the most credible the target can ever be. This lets the user simulate an economy that never reaches full credibility even with stable inflation. The slider is shown inverted, as "Max credibility (1 − θ)", so dragging right means more anchored; the value entering the equation is Blanchard's θ. It moves only when dragged.

  • cred ∈ [0, 1] is a separate stock recording how much of that ceiling is currently realised. It moves on its own, each period, according to how close inflation is to target (6.4).

They multiply:

(6.2)

λ = (1 − θ)·cred Tool addition

λ is the only channel credibility has. It never touches the Phillips-curve slope and never moves Yₙ.

6.3The expectations update

Expectations drift as a convex combination of the target and this period's inflation:

(6.3)

πᵉₜ₊₁ = πᵉₜ + s·[ λₜ(π̄ − πᵉₜ) + (1 − λₜ)(πₜ − πᵉₜ) ]

where s is the step speed. At λ = 1 expectations walk toward the target regardless of what inflation does; at λ = 0 they walk toward realised inflation, which is Blanchard's fully adaptive θ = 1 case. Everything in between is a weighted blend. Collecting terms gives a partial adjustment toward a blended anchor:

πᵉₜ₊₁ = (1 − s)·πᵉₜ + s·[ λₜπ̄ + (1 − λₜ)πₜ ]

which shows at a glance that λ picks the target expectations are heading toward and s picks how fast they get there.

Correspondence with eq. 8.7. Setting s = 1 and cred = 1 gives λ = 1 − θ, so πᵉₜ₊₁ = (1 − θ)π̄ + θπₜ — Blanchard's eq. 8.7 shifted one period. The tool's update is therefore eq. 8.7 with two additions: s < 1 slows the adjustment, and cred < 1 lowers the weight on the target. Both reduce to Blanchard's rule at 1.

Why a convex weight is a legitimate addition. Blanchard makes the same move himself. In his wage-indexation model (§8-4, p. 169), a proportion λ of contracts indexes to actual inflation and (1 − λ) to expected inflation:

(6.4)

π = [λπ + (1 − λ)πᵉ] − α(u − uₙ) (§8-4, p. 169; the equation preceding 8.10) Blanchard

The bracket is a convex weighting of two inflation anchors, and Blanchard notes that as λ rises the same unemployment gap produces ever-larger inflation swings. The tool's credibility factor is this device applied to the expectations equation rather than the Phillips curve: the weight splits the πᵉ update between target and realised inflation, and as credibility falls the weight on the target shrinks — the same amplifying logic, expressed through credibility.

The tool borrows Blanchard's letter deliberately: in both uses λ is a proportion splitting a variable between two anchors — contracts between actual and expected inflation in eq. 8.10, the expectations update between target and realised inflation here.

6.4Credibility: the law of motion

Blanchard has no credibility variable. The tool adds one, cred ∈ [0, 1], which measures how firmly the target is believed and updates each period from the inflation outcome. It is active only while "Allow de-anchoring" is on. Tool addition

Write the inflation gap as gap = |π − π̄|. The rule has two cases.

Inside the band (gap ≤ TOL): credibility rebuilds at a fixed rate.

(6.5)

credₜ₊₁ = min(1, credₜ + REC)

Outside the band (gap > TOL): credibility erodes at a rate that scales with how far past the band inflation has gone.

excess = (gap − TOL)/SCALE

credₜ₊₁ = max(0, credₜ − ERO·excess)

At gap = TOL, excess = 0 and erosion is zero, so the rule is continuous at the edge of the band. One SCALE beyond the band, excess = 1 and credibility falls by the full ERO that period; two beyond, by twice that. Erosion is proportional to the overshoot beyond the band; recovery inside the band is flat. Recovery is slower than full-strength erosion. The asymmetry is the tool's (DEANCHOR_ERO > DEANCHOR_REC), made so that a sustained overshoot has consequences that outlast it; Blanchard's related point is that the risk of de-anchoring grows with the length of time inflation exceeds the target, and falls with the central bank's initial credibility (§9-2, p. 185).

6.5The spiral

The three pieces feed back on each other: cred falls → λ falls → πᵉ tracks realised inflation rather than the target → the Phillips curve shifts up with πᵉ → π stays away from π̄ → cred falls further. This is Blanchard's p. 184 argument made mechanical: the longer inflation sits away from target, the more expectations move, and the harder the central bank's job becomes.

Timing. Both the expectations update and the credibility update read period-t values, so this period's credibility does not affect this period's expectations — the feedback runs with a one-period lag. Each step therefore stays readable as a single comparative-static move.

Values the model usesdefaults and why they were chosen

θ = 0 (slider 0–1, step 0.05; shown as "Max credibility (1 − θ)"). Default is full anchoring, Blanchard's θ = 0, so the full model opens with expectations at target. The inverted label is a display choice only.

cred = 1 (slider 0–1, step 0.05; also shown as a meter). Opens at full credibility. The slider lets the user start a scenario with credibility already damaged.

Step speed s = 0.5 (control, 0.05–1, step 0.05). Half the drift is realised per period. At s = 1 the update is Blanchard's one-step eq. 8.7. The same s governs the price-level and exchange-rate-expectation updates (§8, §9), so it is a global "how much of each period's adjustment happens" dial.

Allow de-anchoring — on by default. With it off, cred is frozen and λ = 1 − θ throughout: the tool is then exactly eq. 8.7 (slowed by s).

The four credibility constants

Constant Value Role
TOL 0.02 tolerance band; inside it credibility recovers
ERO 0.04 erosion rate per period at full strength
REC 0.03 recovery rate per period
SCALE 0.02 how far beyond the band the gap must be for erosion to run at full strength

With both TOL and SCALE at 0.02, full-strength erosion (−0.04 per period) occurs when inflation is 4pp from target; at 3pp it is −0.02; at 6pp, −0.08. TOL and SCALE are numerically equal at present but are separate constants, since one sets where erosion begins and the other how fast it intensifies.

Where these thresholds come from. Blanchard describes de-anchoring qualitatively (§8-3; §9-2). He gives no threshold, no erosion rate, and no recovery condition. The tool needs them to run, so TOL, SCALE, ERO and REC are tool calibrations, chosen so that the mechanism is visible over the ten to twenty periods a student will step through. They are not estimates and have no textbook counterpart. What the tool takes from Blanchard is the idea: sustained deviation from target de-anchors expectations, the risk growing with the duration of the overshoot and falling with initial credibility (§9-2, p. 185; §8-3 for the 1970s and Covid contrast). The stock itself, its recovery condition, the threshold and the feedback into πᵉ are the tool's. Richer treatments make de-anchoring endogenous, with agents re-estimating the inflation process each period rather than crossing a fixed threshold (Evans and Honkapohja, 2001); the tool's threshold version trades that realism for a mechanism a student can watch operate on a chart.

Clamps. πᵉ is held within [−10%, 60%]; cred within [0, 1]. Numerical guards; the first never binds in normal use.

7Net exports

Textbook anchor: Ch. 18 (Problem 7; §18-4, p. 384; Appendix, p. 393), Ch. 19 (eq. 19.1, p. 396; eq. 19.2)

The equation the tool uses:

(7.4)

NX = x₁Y* − m₁Y − n₁(ε − 1) Blanchard, linearised

7.1What Blanchard gives

Net exports pose two problems for the tool. First, Blanchard gives no full linear form; only the functional form

(7.1)

NX(Y, Y*, ε) = X(Y*, ε) − IM(Y, ε)/ε (eq. 19.1) Blanchard

in which imports are measured in foreign goods and divided by ε to convert them to domestic-good units. Second, the one linear form he does give (Ch. 18, Problem 7),

(7.2)

IM = m₁Y, X = x₁Y*

has no exchange-rate term — and not by oversight: the problem instructs the reader to hold the real exchange rate fixed at 1, so the ε-channel is switched off by construction. In the tool ε is dynamic, so the channel has to be built.

Its sign Blanchard does give. For the trade balance to improve after a real depreciation, exports must rise and imports fall by enough to outweigh the higher price of imports — the Marshall–Lerner condition (§18-4, p. 384), derived formally in the chapter appendix (p. 393). He reports that it holds in reality and assumes it for the rest of the book: a real depreciation (a fall in ε) raises net exports. The tool assumes it too.

7.2Linearising in ε

A first-order Taylor expansion replaces a smooth curve near a point a by its tangent there:

f(ε) ≈ f(a) + f′(a)·(ε − a)

The tool expands around a = 1, the real exchange rate at which the model loads. So

NX(ε) ≈ NX|₍ε₌₁₎ + (dNX/dε)|₍ε₌₁₎ · (ε − 1)

and two things are needed: the height NX(1) and the slope NX′(1). Take the slope first, one component at a time.

Exports. X depends on Y* and ε, so

dX/dε = ∂X/∂Y* · dY*/dε + ∂X/∂ε

Y* is foreign output and exogenous — the domestic economy is small relative to the rest of the world, so foreign output does not respond to the domestic exchange rate — hence dY*/dε = 0 and

dX/dε = ∂X/∂ε < 0

A real appreciation makes home goods dearer abroad.

Imports. The object is the import bill in domestic goods, B(ε) = IM(Y, ε)/ε, not the volume IM. For the numerator, IM depends on Y and ε, so

dIM/dε = ∂IM/∂ε + ∂IM/∂Y · dY/dε

Y is held fixed for this partial: what is being linearised is the NX function at a given level of output. The feedback by which a depreciation raises Y and higher Y pulls in more imports enters later, through the multiplier 1/(1 − c₁ − b₁ + m₁) of §2.6. Keeping the two separate is what lets the IS equation be assembled from parts. So dY/dε = 0 here and

dIM/dε = ∂IM/∂ε > 0

An appreciation makes foreign goods cheaper at home, so more are bought. Now the quotient rule on B = IM/ε, with (u/v)′ = (u′v − uv′)/v²:

dB/dε = (∂IM/∂ε · ε − IM · 1)/ε²

dB/dε = (∂IM/∂ε)/ε − IM/ε²

One term has become two, and that is the point of the step. ε was doing two jobs in B — inside IM as a driver of quantity, and underneath as the divisor — and differentiation separates them:

  • (∂IM/∂ε)/ε is the volume effect, positive: an appreciation means more goods imported, so the bill rises.
  • −IM/ε² is the valuation effect, negative: the same appreciation makes each imported good cheaper in domestic-good units, so the bill falls.

They pull against each other. Before differentiating, the ÷ε looks like a passive scaling factor; afterwards it is visibly a separate economic channel with its own sign.

Rebuilding NX. By the difference rule,

dNX/dε = ∂X/∂ε − [ (∂IM/∂ε)/ε − IM/ε² ]

dNX/dε = ∂X/∂ε − (∂IM/∂ε)/ε + IM/ε²

At ε = 1 both divisors are 1:

dNX/dε|₍ε₌₁₎ = ∂X/∂ε − ∂IM/∂ε + IM

These are exactly Blanchard's three channels from §18-4: export volume (∂X/∂ε < 0), import volume (−∂IM/∂ε < 0), and valuation (+IM > 0). To match Blanchard's text, the slope is named with a minus sign so that it is positive under Marshall–Lerner:

(7.3)

n₁ ≡ −(dNX/dε)|₍ε₌₁₎ = −∂X/∂ε + ∂IM/∂ε − IM, so dNX/dε|₍ε₌₁₎ = −n₁

Marshall–Lerner is now simply n₁ > 0: the two volume effects outweigh the valuation effect. A reader might ask where the ÷ε has gone; it has been absorbed into n₁.

The height. Evaluating eq. 19.1 at ε = 1, with the Problem 7 linear forms:

NX|₍ε₌₁₎ = X(Y*, 1) − IM(Y, 1)/1 = x₁Y* − m₁Y

Assembling. Both pieces back into the expansion, with a = 1:

NX(ε) ≈ NX(1) + NX′(1)·(ε − 1)

NX ≈ (x₁Y* − m₁Y) + (−n₁)(ε − 1)

NX = x₁Y* − m₁Y − n₁(ε − 1) Blanchard, linearised

The intercept is the baseline trade balance; the entire ε-response is carried by one constant. A real depreciation (ε < 1) makes −n₁(ε − 1) positive and improves net exports, as Marshall–Lerner requires. At ε = 1 the last term vanishes and NX collapses to x₁Y* − m₁Y — Problem 7's set-up exactly, which is the reassurance that the linearisation is centred where the textbook problem sits.

7.3Into the IS

Substituting into eq. 19.1 and collecting the −m₁Y term with the other Y terms gives the open multiplier k_o = 1/(1 − c₁ − b₁ + m₁) and the trade term x₁Y* − n₁(ε − 1) in the IS bracket, as set out in §2.6.

7.4What the linear form leaves out

Two separate simplifications are involved, and it is worth keeping them apart.

The first is static. The true relation between net exports and ε is a curve, not a line: dNX/dε varies with the level of ε. The expansion replaces the curve with its tangent at ε = 1, so n₁ is fixed at its baseline value and used at every ε. The discarded term is second-order, so the error grows with the square of the distance from 1: close near the baseline, degrading for a very large depreciation. This is one reason the tool's ε range is kept near 1. In the tool itself the linear form is exact by construction, because the engine defines NX this way rather than approximating a curved function.

The second is dynamic, and the linearisation does not address it at all. Trade volumes adjust more slowly than prices: contracts are already signed and buyers take time to switch suppliers, so immediately after a depreciation the volume responses are small and the valuation effect — a higher import bill for a given quantity — can dominate. Net exports worsen before they improve: the J-curve. It arises because the trade responses differ between the short and long run, not because the relation is curved in ε. A single constant n₁ cannot reproduce it: one coefficient has one sign, so the tool's net exports move in the correct direction at once and the initial deterioration never appears.

Both are deliberate. Economo is built to display the linear relationships of the textbook model, in which Blanchard likewise assumes throughout that a real depreciation improves net exports. What is retained is the sign and the long-run magnitude of the response, which is what eq. 19.2 requires under Marshall–Lerner.

8Uncovered interest parity and the real exchange rate

Textbook anchor: Ch. 17 (p. 357; eqs. 17.1, 17.2, 17.4), Ch. 19 (eqs. 19.2, 19.3, 19.5, p. 397), Ch. 20 (§20-3, eq. 20.5, pp. 425–428)

(8.2)

8.1Uncovered interest parity

Investors arbitrage between domestic and foreign bonds, equalising expected returns:

(8.1)

(1 + i) = (1 + i*)·(E/Eᵉ) (eq. 17.2 / 19.3) Blanchard

Solving for the current exchange rate, with the expected future rate Eᵉ taken as given for the period:

E = Eᵉ·(1 + i)/(1 + i*) (eq. 19.5) Blanchard

The tool's relation is eq. 19.5 verbatim — the cleanest match in the model. A higher domestic rate appreciates the currency (E rises); a higher foreign rate depreciates it. Blanchard's approximate form (eq. 17.4), i ≈ i* − (Eᵉ − E)/E, is the same relation read the other way: the domestic rate equals the foreign rate minus the expected appreciation. The tool uses the exact form.

Blanchard defines E as the price of the domestic currency in terms of foreign currency (p. 357), so a rise in E is an appreciation — the opposite of the convention in much of the press, and worth remembering when reading the chart.

8.2From nominal to real

UIP fixes the nominal exchange rate E, but the goods market responds to the real exchange rate ε — the relative price of domestic goods in terms of foreign goods. The two are linked by the price levels:

(8.3)

ε = E·P/P* (eq. 17.1, p. 358) Blanchard

This closes the loop into the IS curve: UIP sets E from the interest differential; the price ratio P/P* converts it to ε; and ε enters net exports through the −n₁(ε − 1) term of §7. In Blanchard's short-run simplification (eq. 19.2, p. 397) the price levels are fixed at P = P* = 1 so that ε = E. The tool does exactly this in the pre-Phillips-curve stages, where prices are constant (§9): there ε and E coincide, and the tool is eq. 19.2. Once the Phillips-curve block is active both price levels move, ε and E diverge, and inflation differentials become a source of real appreciation and depreciation (§9).

8.3The expected exchange rate

Blanchard takes Eᵉ as constant in Ch. 19 and says in §20-3 that this was a simplification. Applying UIP to next period and the one after, he solves forward (eq. 20.5, p. 427): today's exchange rate depends on the whole path of current and expected future interest differentials, and on the exchange rate expected far in the future. His example (p. 428) makes the point: a rate cut expected to last one year depreciates the currency by about 2%; the same cut expected to last five years, by about 10%. How expectations respond to the change in the rate determines the size of the effect.

The tool cannot ask the user for a ten-year path of expected rates, so it lets Eᵉ adjust to what it observes:

(8.4)

Eᵉₜ₊₁ = (1 − 0.4s)·Eᵉₜ + 0.4s·Eₜ Tool addition

where s is the step speed (§6). Each period expectations close a fraction 0.4s of the gap to the realised rate — one fifth at s = 0.5. This is the same partial-adjustment form as the expectations rule of §6 and the smoothing of §5. It reproduces Blanchard's conclusion in slow motion: in Ch. 20 the full effect of a persistent differential arrives at once, because investors reason forward; in the tool it accrues period by period as Eᵉ catches up with E, so a differential that lasts moves the exchange rate more than one that does not. A steady state requires Eᵉ = E and hence i = i*; while the domestic rate stays above the foreign rate the currency keeps appreciating slowly (about 0.2% per period at a 1pp differential and s = 0.5).

The tool has no forward-looking Eᵉ, so it has no news effect and no forward solution; what it reproduces is the comparative result of §20-3 (pp. 427–428) — a differential expected to last moves the exchange rate more — by accumulation rather than anticipation. This is also why a permanent change in the world interest rate works through so slowly here: the whole adjustment has to accumulate through Eᵉ, a fraction of the gap each period, where Blanchard's forward-looking investors price it in at once.

9Price-level dynamics

Textbook anchor: Ch. 19 (eq. 19.2, p. 397), Ch. 17 (ε = EP/P*)

The equations the tool uses each period, once the Phillips-curve block is active:

(9.1)

Pₜ₊₁ = Pₜ·(1 + s·πₜ) Tool addition

(9.2)

P*ₜ₊₁ = P*ₜ·(1 + s·π̄) Tool addition

Before the Phillips-curve block is active, P = P* = 1 throughout: Blanchard's eq. 19.2.

9.1Why the tool needs price levels

Blanchard's Chapter 19 fixes both price levels at 1, which is right for his short run: with P and P* constant the real and nominal exchange rates coincide and the analysis can be done in E alone. The tool's medium run has inflation, and inflation is a rate of change of the price level. Once π is determined by the Phillips curve each period, P must accumulate it, or the real exchange rate would ignore the inflation the tool itself generates. So the tool goes one step beyond eq. 19.2 and lets both price levels evolve.

9.2The two updates

The domestic price level grows at actual domestic inflation, scaled by the step speed:

(9.1)

Pₜ₊₁ = Pₜ·(1 + s·πₜ)

The foreign price level grows at the inflation target:

(9.2)

P*ₜ₊₁ = P*ₜ·(1 + s·π̄)

The foreign central bank is treated as permanently on target — the same assumption as i* sitting at its neutral rate (§8, Values). Nothing in the tool moves foreign inflation.

9.3What follows for the real exchange rate

Substituting into ε = E·P/P*, real appreciation and depreciation are driven by the inflation differential π − π̄. When domestic inflation runs above target, P outpaces P*, ε rises (a real appreciation) and net exports fall through the n₁ channel of §7 — the mechanism behind the divergence between the ε and E lines on the exchange-rate chart, and the reason a fiscal expansion in the full model is partly crowded out through trade as well as through the interest rate. When π = π̄, both price levels grow in lockstep and ε moves only with the nominal rate E.

This is the medium-run counterpart of Blanchard's fixed-price short run: in Ch. 19 the real exchange rate moves only because E moves; in the tool it also moves because prices do, which is what makes the open-economy Phillips-curve stage different from the closed one.
